## Key points (ch. 99, by Tom Masiero — Great American Mining / Standard Bitcoin)

- Definitions: "Stranded gas refers to natural gas deposits that, due to their remote location or low production volumes, aren't economically viable to transport to the market using traditional infrastructure. Flared gas, on the other hand, is a byproduct of oil production that is either vented or, more often, burned off into the atmosphere for safety reasons, mainly to manage pressure levels and reduce the risk of explosions." (p. 99)
- Industry structure: oil and gas divides into "upstream, midstream, and downstream sectors" — "Upstream refers to the exploration and extraction of oil and gas; midstream denotes the processing, storage, and transportation; and downstream encompasses the refining of petroleum crude oil and the processing and purification of raw natural gas." (p. 100)
- Scale of the resource: "It is estimated that as much as 30% of the over 7,257 trillion cubic feet of known natural gas reserves on earth are stranded"; for context, "in 2022, the USA consumed 32.31 trillion cubic feet of natural gas." (p. 101)
- Bakken flaring: "The U.S. Energy Information Agency reports that around 19% of all natural gas produced in the state in 2019 was flared, the equivalent of about 0.56 billion cubic feet per day." (p. 101)
- Pipeline economics: "in 2020, new pipeline construction costs would average $7.65 million per mile. For a stranded gas field 100 miles from the nearest pipeline, the cost of constructing a new connection would be $765 million—not a small sum." (p. 102)
- Regulatory cost: "in the first ten months of 2022, oil & gas companies in Nigeria were fined a total of $341 million for flaring. The fines range from between $0.5-$2 per thousand cubic feet (Mcf)... With a current gas price of around $2.7 per Mcf, it's clear that the financial impact of fines can quickly surpass the value of the flared gas itself." (p. 102)
- Decentralization property: "Off-grid flare deployments typically have less than a megawatt of capacity, which makes off-grid mining the most decentralized hashrate on the bitcoin network." (p. 103)
- Gas pricing history: "Back when we first started, it was common to get the gas for free, and in some cases, miners even got paid to take the gas! Nowadays, miners typically pay an average of $1mcf/d, which is roughly equivalent to $0.01 kw/h." (p. 103)
- Generator economics: renting units "from service companies like Mesa, Moser, or Baseline Energy... it was common to be able to rent a generator for less than $0.03 kw/h, that allowed off-grid miners to mine bitcoin in the $0.04-0.05 kw/h range—a very competitive rate"; owning generation drives OpEx "to only pay for maintenance and gas". (p. 104)
- Dual exposure: "miners are exposed to not only the dynamics of bitcoin's volatility but also the market conditions of Oil and Gas producers. In the spring of 2020, Oil and Gas prices went negative at one point and forced many operations... to turn off" — during a period when "the price had crashed to the $4,000 range" after a new halving epoch. (p. 104)
- Hardware lessons: GAM chose "the Whatsminer M20s model, which was best in class at the time"; hindsight lesson: "lower-cost, older-generation mining machines could provide a buffer against market fluctuations." (pp. 103, 106)
- Business models seen 2019–2021: "From a straight deal that would consist of an off-take agreement for the gas to sophisticated revenue share deals with producers and even generator service providers that wanted exposure to bitcoin." (p. 105)
- Standard Bitcoin approach: bought "Upstream Data's Hash Huts... basically a generator and mining unit in one box. This allowed us to deliver the hash hut to our gas wells, plug-in and start hashing." (p. 105)
- Remote ops: "With the advent of Starlink, bitcoin miners can quickly set up mining farms in remote locations. Get yourself a $200 laptop from Amazon and you can remotely manage the hash hut as well." (p. 105)
- Case studies: "1. Great American Mining (GAM), an early pioneer of off-grid mining" (vertical integration for well-pad safety); "2. Standard Bitcoin... opted for Hashhuts from Upstream Data"; "3. Crusoe Energy... deploy modular data centers directly at oil well sites, converting flared gas into electricity onsite." (pp. 105–107)
- Field rules (verbatim): "1. NEVER host for off-grid. (Uptime % can vary based on factors outside your control. You will get REKT) 2. NEVER EVER buy machines in a bull market. Trust me and thank me later." (p. 108)
- Climate caveat: "Heat is NOT a miner's friend, especially the West Texas heat. Machines tend to have their lifespan affected in these environments." (p. 105)
