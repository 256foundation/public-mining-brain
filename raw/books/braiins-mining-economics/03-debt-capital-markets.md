## Key points (ch. 32–45 — History of Debt in Bitcoin Mining: Part 1, by Drew Armstrong)

- Why early mining had no debt: hardware useful life was "very short, with certain models remaining competitive for only 6 – 18 months"; returns were "highly volatile"; "annual mining revenue didn't exceed $1 billion until 2017"; the industry "was still immature". (pp. 32–33)
- Retail bitcoin-backed debt arrived "until 2017... en masse" via "Unchained Capital and BlockFi, while trading houses like Genesis and Galaxy Digital provided the early collateralized debt products for institutions." (p. 33)
- First public miners (2017/2018): "Riot Blockchain (which pivoted from a pharmaceutical technology company to bitcoin mining in 2017), DMG Blockchain, Hive Blockchain (which pivoted from a minerals exploration company in 2017), Hut 8, and Fortress Blockchain (now Cathedra Bitcoin)"; Bitfarms went public in 2019, Marathon "completing its pivot in 2020". (p. 33)
- "In 2018 Galaxy Digital issued a $16m term loan to Hut 8 to finance new mining infrastructure, collateralized by bitcoin and infrastructure"; ASIC-backed debt pioneers Arctos Capital and Blockfills founded 2018; Foundry (2019) and Galaxy Digital Mining (2020) followed. (p. 34)
- Growth enablers: commoditized hardware ("By summer 2019, the hardware market was dominated by Bitmain, Canaan, and MicroBT"); "The Bitmain Antminer S9 was first released in 2016 but was still in wide use up until the 2020 halvening"; "pockets of yield-starved capital salivated at the prospect of 8 – 30% debt". (pp. 34–35)
- 2021 excess: with ASIC "futures orders" it "even became possible to secure debt financing on machines that had not yet been delivered"; new entrants launched "multi-billion-dollar SPACs on zero deployed hash rate." (p. 36)
- Capital stack seniority: "senior debt, junior or mezzanine debt, preferred equity, and equity"; debt holders are "contractually senior" and "paid out first in the event of a bankruptcy or liquidation." (p. 38)
- LTV logic: "if a lender had to foreclose on an 80% LTV loan and liquidate the collateral, the asset price could fall by 20% before the lender took a hit. In other words, the higher the LTV, the riskier the loan." (p. 40)
- Unsecured loans are "rarely available to bitcoin miners." (p. 40)
- Pricing mantra (Art Lyon): "There's no bad risk, just a bad price." (p. 40)
- Bitcoin-backed debt: interest rates "ranging from 4 – 12%"; loans "typically don't amortize"; margin call provisions when "bitcoin falls below a certain price". (p. 42)
- ASIC-backed debt: "typically had 18-month terms for an all-in cost of capital ranging from 13 – 28%"; loans amortize once machines are plugged in; some lenders offered "low LTVs (sub 50% in some cases), but even still, the market turmoil of H2 2022 has resulted in many such loans becoming undercollateralized (LTVs > 100%)"; covenants might "prohibit the use of firmware and immersion". (pp. 42–43)
- Structure comparison table (p. 45): BTC-backed cost of capital "4–12%"; ASIC-backed "13–28%" with "18 months" typical term; infrastructure-backed "5–25%"; senior secured loans "varies".

## Key points (ch. 46–61 — Debt Capital Markets in Bitcoin Mining: Part 2, by Drew Armstrong)

- Lender's primary goal: "to earn attractive risk-adjusted returns ('return on capital'). This means AT LEAST receiving their principal back ('return OF capital')". (p. 46)
- Loan sizing economics: "the work required to originate a $1 million loan vs. a $100 million loan is surprisingly similar; thus, lenders often have minimum loan sizes". (p. 47)
- Hidden borrower costs: "Upfront or origination fees: typically 1 - 2%"; warrants; related transaction fees (e.g., hedging). (p. 50)
- Collateral restrictions: "loans with ASIC collateral often prevent the use of aftermarket firmware or advanced cooling technologies (e.g., immersion) without lender approval" — to preserve collateral value and secondary-market fungibility. (p. 50)
- Case study setup: "a new project is built for $2.4m"; "1,000 S19J Pros purchased at $15 / TH ($1.5m in total)"; "Supporting infrastructure costs $300k / MW ($900k in total for an assume 3MW site)"; each S19J Pro "pulls 3,000 watts"; 3-year forecast; "95% uptime and $55 / MWh all-in operating costs". (p. 52)
- Loan structure: "an ASIC-backed loan with a ~50% LTV ($750k of debt proceeds, prior to a 2% origination fee). We assume this $765k loan has an interest rate of 15% and an 18 month term." (p. 52)
- Base case: hash price "~6.5 cents / TH / s / day" — "It takes nearly the entire 3 year period for the miner to recover their initial equity investment." (p. 53)
- Bear case: hash price "5.0 cents (the approximate hash price if bitcoin fell to $15.3k and the network held at 280 EH/s)" — "the levered miner never recovering more than 14% of its invested capital, while the unlevered miner earns only 45% of the invested capital." (p. 54)
- Distress paths: "Typically, one or more of the following would occur: restructuring, default, or bankruptcy." (p. 54)
- Bull case: bitcoin back to "its previous all time high of $69,420" → "~23 cent hash price"; "the levered miner earns a return on their invested capital 3x higher than that of the unlevered miner." (pp. 55–56)
- TLDR (verbatim): "leverage offers miners the ability to amplify returns and expand faster, but it comes at the cost of fragility during bear markets." (p. 56)
- Why ASIC-backed debt struggles: correlation ("as bitcoin price falls, so too do ASIC prices (the collateral value), and the profit margins of miners"), volatility ("both witnessed ~80%+ drawdowns"), and thin ASIC liquidity ("ASIC orderbooks can be quite thin"). (pp. 57–58)
- Rate sensitivity: "a ~5% increase in ASIC-backed debt could take interest rates as high as 20-30%"; "bitcoin is the best form of collateral in human history." (p. 58)
- Hashrate financing: "Hash rate financing is non-dilutive (similar to debt)" and "can also allow miners to hedge some of their future production. Famously, hash rate marketplaces have been 6 - 12 months away for the last 3 or so years." (p. 60)
- Aftermath: "many lenders have left the asset class either due to poor loan performance or because they've gone out of business altogether (e.g., BlockFi and Celsius)." (p. 61)
