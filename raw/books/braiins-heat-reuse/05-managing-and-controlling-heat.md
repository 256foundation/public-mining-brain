## Key points (ch. 5)

- Digital heat thesis: "These systems are digital – they're computers that mine bitcoin – and because they use electricity to generate heat, they can be optimized specifically for high-performance and highly controllable heat." (p. 74)
- Bang-bang control defined: "Most traditional heating systems – furnaces, boilers, water heaters, and some space heaters – use a type of control called 'bang-bang' (or on-off) control, cycling the heater on and off to maintain a desired temperature." Example: 70°F target, kicks on around 68°F, often overshooting to 72°F. (p. 74)
- Bang-bang constraint: "partial power settings are not an option – you can't burn natural gas or propane at 'half-power'." (p. 75)
- Bang-bang headroom advantage: "a propane furnace can be designed to produce 500,000 BTU/hr as easily as 100,000 BTU/hr, by simply adjusting its duty cycle to deliver the right amount of heat. This means bang-bang systems are well-equipped to handle extreme cold snaps without extra cost." (p. 75)
- Continuous load defined: "Bitcoin miners, in contrast, can operate continuously and precisely control heat output by adjusting the power they consume. Since 100% of the mining power converts to heat, adjusting the miner's power directly changes the amount of heat produced." (p. 76)
- Modulation example: "On a 30°F day, 1,500 watts (5,118 BTU/hr) might be enough, while a colder day at 15°F might require 2,100 watts (7,165 BTU/hr). The miner can dynamically adjust its power in real time to maintain the set temperature." (p. 76)
- Stability advantage: "well-designed hashrate heating systems maintain a stable target temperature, providing greater comfort and reliability – ideal for homes, offices, and applications needing precise temperature control." (p. 76)
- Headroom drawback: "tripling the capacity to handle extreme cold would require three times as many mining chips. This makes extra hashrate heating capacity an expensive solution compared to bang-bang systems." (p. 77)
- Two things to manage: "There are two things to manage in a bitcoin-powered heating system: the amount of heat and where the heat goes." Both manageable "with software or analog controls, or a combination of the two." (p. 77)
- Amount of heat: "This control is handled through software and firmware... you can adjust the power draw of a miner, either by overclocking or underclocking, to meet specific heating needs." (p. 78)
- Where heat goes: smart electronic components "can be programmatically controlled... combined with a smart home system"; or analog — "a temperature regulating valve can modulate water temperature by mixing hot and cold streams without needing any electricity"; or manual levers on valves and dampers. (p. 79)
- Constant mining vs on-heat demand mining: constant mining runs "full-time (maximum uptime)... the heat produced is either reused when needed or rejected when it's not"; on-heat demand mining means "you only mine bitcoin when you need heat... the miners are treated like traditional heating systems." (pp. 79–80)
- Reject/Reuse framing: "Reject / Reuse hashrate heating systems are essentially bitcoin mining operations with an integrated heating solution baked in." (p. 81)
- Dominant mode: "Due to energy prices, most systems will opt for hashing for heat only." (p. 81)
- Analog control strengths: "known for their simplicity, reliability, and long-lasting durability... they provide a reliable, set-and-forget approach to heat management." (p. 82)
- Software control strengths: "software control uses programmable settings and logic via written code to fine-tune heat output and efficiency... It also allows for remote adjustments and real-time monitoring." (pp. 82–83)
- Hybrid safety pattern: "you could set up analog relays to act as fail-safes, automatically cutting power if temperatures get too high, while the software takes care of everyday adjustments and overall heat regulation." (p. 83)
- Complexity ladder: all-analog → "basic firmware control, letting you tweak miner power settings" → "software-driven systems like Home Assistant on a microcontroller to create granular, smart control." (pp. 83–84)
- Aftermarket firmware for heat: "With custom firmware, like Braiins OS, miners can ramp their power from 100% throttle to very low levels, offering a broad range of heating capacity." — "Braiins' Autotuning feature optimizes the miner's efficiency at non-default power levels, allowing the hashrate heater to maximize rewards at any power and heat limit." (p. 84)
- Dynamic Performance Scaling (DPS): "Firmware solutions like Braiins OS with Dynamic Performance Scaling (DPS)... allow users to set temperature limits for the machine's internal temperature sensors. If they start to overheat, the miner automatically lowers its own power draw (and heat output) by a defined amount" — "no additional hardware or coding required." (p. 85)
- DPS limitation: "you likely don't care about the temperature inside your hashrate heater – you're focused on the actual temperature of your heating application, like rooms in a house." — "a household thermostat doesn't measure the temperature inside the furnace or boiler." (pp. 85–86)
- External-sensor thermostats: solutions "connect via API (application programming interface) to aftermarket firmware tools like Braiins OS. The Thermohashostat from 100AcresRanch, for example, works with other aftermarket firmware, while devices like the Heatmine miner thermostat integrate with Braiins OS." (pp. 86–87)
- Maximum analog: miner at full power on stock firmware, "turned on or off using a switch relay that controls power to the miner power supply"; analog pumps/fans wired to the miner's power circuit; temperature-regulating valves direct heat "all without digital intervention." (p. 87)
- Full smart stack: "A bitcoin miner is equipped with Braiins OS... The miner connects to a microcontroller that allows remote adjustments. Software like Home Assistant can then link other smart devices together, enabling thermostatic control based on external temperature sensors"; "analog relays and switches are often included as safety backups... they can cut power to the hashrate heater in the event of a pump failure or loss of pressure." (p. 88)
- Open-source exemplars: Nakamoto Heating's "HashTub" by Michael Schmid ("a comprehensive parts list, schematics, and video documentation... along with a GitHub repository"); the Wi-Fi-enabled PDU from Toine at 256 Heat enables "full system shutdown when heat isn't needed, rather than just putting the miner in a sleep state – which can still draw significant power on certain firmwares." (p. 89)
- Immersion thermal battery: "The immersion tank acts as a thermal battery, storing heat energy due to the high thermal mass of the liquid... When the oil cools down sufficiently, the miners turn on to warm it back up; if the oil overheats, they switch off. This approach, designed by Heatpunk Cody Harris" — "heat is immediately available thanks to the stored energy in the oil tank." (pp. 90–91)
- Efficiency vs control trade: "Most miners have a hashing efficiency 'sweet spot,' often slightly below their rated power limit. Bang-bang control, cycling on and off at this level, might yield slightly better J/TH efficiency... However, this approach can cause faster wear and tear on the hardware and larger temperature fluctuations." (p. 91)
- Future modulation: "If miners become capable of dynamic, real-time tuning to maximize efficiency at any power level – something companies like Auradine are pursuing with their latest models – then continuous control could become the standard." (p. 91)
