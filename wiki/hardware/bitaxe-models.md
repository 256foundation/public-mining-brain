## Overview

The Bitaxe is skot's open-source, single-board Bitcoin ASIC miner: one (or a few) Bitmain hashing chips salvaged from a current-generation Antminer, an ESP32-S3 Wi-Fi microcontroller running ESP-Miner/AxeOS, and a small 5V power stage, all on one PCB. The OSMU wiki describes the classic single-chip board as a standalone miner at 250-500 gigahashes per second using up to 15 watts ([OSMU wiki: Bitaxe](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/osmu-wiki-bitaxe-about.md)). Each generation moves to a newer chip — BM1397 (Max) → BM1366 (Ultra) → BM1368 (Supra) → BM1370 (Gamma) — and the family has since grown multi-chip boards (Hex, GT, Gamma Hex), next-gen prototypes (Naja, Naja Duo) and Intel BZM2 research boards (BIRDS, Bonanza) ([foss-miner-list](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/github-bitaxeorg-foss-miner-list.md); [ecosystem discovery](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-github-ecosystem-discovery.md)). Hardware is copyleft (CERN-OHL-S) and firmware GPL-3.0, so a large derivative ecosystem (NerdAxe, NerdQAxe, PiAxe, Qaxe, BitForge, TinyChipHub) exists alongside closed-source clones (LuckyMiner and others) that the maintainers refuse to support. Buyers are steered to the community-vetted legitlist. In OpenSats' 2026-04-21 report skot estimated over 100,000 Bitaxe units sold — an estimate, not a sales ledger ([OpenSats impact report](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/2026-04-21-opensats-bitaxe-impact-report.md)). Through 2025-2026 the POD256 podcast tracked newer boards that the repos record only partly: the Bitaxe Raw USB pass-through firmware, a working four-chip Bitaxe Bonanza, the Bitaxe Proto on Block's Proto ASIC, and African-built forks (BitShaka and the WhatsMiner-chip Bitaxe Nini). See [Newer and experimental boards](#newer-and-experimental-boards).

## Origin and design philosophy

- **"Dayminer."** On POD256 e117 (2026-06-17) Scott said the Bitaxe was first imagined as a board mounted on the back of a solar panel that would join Wi-Fi and mine whenever the sun was up. That is why it was called Dayminer before it was called Bitaxe ([POD256 e117](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-06-17-e117.md)).
- **Starting point.** When Scott went looking for an open ASIC miner design he found none. He found only a blog post by someone who had partly reverse-engineered the Antminer S1 chip over serial, which showed that the chips take block headers over a serial link ([POD256 e087](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-09-17-e087.md)). On POD256 e089 (2025-10-05) the host dated the first GitHub push under the Bitaxe name to May 2022 and put the installed base at "tens of thousands" ([POD256 e089](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-10-05-e089.md)). Scott's lineage slide on e103 runs: original Bitaxe (S9 chip), Ultra, Supra, Gamma, Gamma Turbo; then the NerdQAxe, NerdQAxe+ and NerdQAxe++; with the Octaxe as a license-respecting community fork ([POD256 e103](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-02-04-e103.md)).
- **Why it spread.** Hosts on e109 put the Bitaxe's commercial success down to the ESP32 sitting on the hashboard. With no separate control board, manufacturers can copy the design straight into production ([POD256 e109](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-03-25-e109.md)). Hosts on e112 framed its value as the first iteration of open-source mining hardware, not as hashrate or cost per terahash. It removed the need for high-voltage outlets, venting and sound enclosures ([POD256 e112](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-04-15-e112.md)).
- **Unauthorized parts.** Scott noted on e106 that every Bitaxe and heat-reuse build uses "essentially unauthorized parts" with no support from the ASIC maker ([POD256 e106](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-03-04-e106.md)). Bitmain binning varies widely: per Scott, the same BM1370 part can do almost two terahash or about half a terahash per chip ([POD256 e121](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-07-27-e121.md)). He also said Bitmain released a lower-hashrate BM1370 variant with no distinctive marking, so buyers can tell it apart only by the miner it came from ([POD256 e105](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-02-18-e105.md)).

## Model numbering

- **Series = hundreds digit, revision = the rest.** Board numbers identify both the model family and the PCB revision: Max is 10x, Ultra 20x, Ultra Hex 30x, Supra 40x, Gamma 60x, Gamma Duo 65x, TinyChipHub Supra Hex / Gamma Hex 70x, GT 80x and the bitaxeorg Gamma Hex 130x ([foss-miner-list](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/github-bitaxeorg-foss-miner-list.md)). An open ESP-Miner proposal to burn the board version into ESP32-S3 eFuse spells out the same convention: hundreds digit for board type, remainder for revision ([ESP-Miner #716](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2025-02-18-github-esp-miner-issue-716-migrate-board-version-to-efuse.md)).
- **OSMU wiki page names** use a round number plus nickname: 100 'Max', 200 'Ultra', 300 'Hex', 400 'Supra', 600 'Gamma', 650 'Gamma Duo', 801 'Gamma Turbo' ([OSMU wiki: Bitaxe 100](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/osmu-wiki-bitaxe-100.md); [OSMU wiki: Bitaxe 801](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/osmu-wiki-bitaxe-801.md)).
- **Pre-scheme boards.** Board version 0.11 is an early Ultra from before the current numbering; its closest modern equivalent is 204. Flashing with the wrong board version produced failures like a phantom ASIC temperature and no mining ([ESP-Miner #94](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2024-01-21-github-esp-miner-issue-94-about-board-version-distinction.md); [ESP-Miner #178](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2024-05-22-github-esp-miner-issue-178-won-t-boot-screen-blank-after-upd.md)).
- **The board version matters at flash time.** ESP-Miner keys its device config (regulator, fan controller, ASIC enable pin) off the board version in NVS; for example, 204 and 205 differ only in the `asic_enable` pin, and a board flashed with the 204 NVS file never hashed on 2.13.1 ([ESP-Miner #1612](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2026-03-14-github-esp-miner-issue-1612-asic-disabled-by-default-for-the.md)).
- **ASIC ↔ model map** from the AxeOS `/api/system/asic` docs: BM1397 = original Bitaxe (Max), BM1366 = Ultra, BM1368 = Supra, BM1370 = Gamma ([OSMU wiki: API](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/osmu-wiki-bitaxe-api.md)).

> **Status: Disputed** — which Bitmain chip the Naja carries
> The Naja README lists 2x BM1340 as a suspected S23 prototype chip, and the Naja Duo README lists 2x BM1373. On POD256 e117 (2026-06-17) Scott said the rumored S23 chip is a BM1373 whose chip-ID register reportedly returns BM1372. ### Which "major revision" is which?

> **Status: Disputed** — revision count
> bitaxe.org calls Ultra the 2nd, Supra the 3rd and Gamma the 4th major revision ([bitaxe.org hardware](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/bitaxe-org-hardware.md)). The bitaxeUltra README calls Ultra the 3rd ([bitaxeUltra README](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/github-bitaxeorg-bitaxeultra.md)); the bitaxeSupra README and the OSMU wiki call Supra the 4th ([bitaxeSupra README](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/github-bitaxeorg-bitaxesupra.md); [OSMU wiki: Bitaxe 400](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/osmu-wiki-bitaxe-400.md)); the bitaxeGamma README calls Gamma the 5th ([bitaxeGamma README](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/github-bitaxeorg-bitaxegamma.md)); and the OSMU wiki calls Gamma the 6th ([OSMU wiki: Bitaxe 600](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/osmu-wiki-bitaxe-600.md)). Best assessment: the counts differ by whether the BM1387 prototype, the BM1397 Max and the Ultra Hex are counted as revisions. bitaxe.org's count (Max = 1st) is the shortest and most recent marketing copy; the READMEs appear to count the BM1387 prototype as well. Use model names and board numbers, not ordinal revision counts.

## Board revisions and quirks

Per-board BOM/footprint errata and fixes are collected in [Bitaxe Board Design & Errata](bitaxe-board-design-and-errata.md); this section covers what a buyer or flasher needs to know.

- **Ultra.** 204 is an Ultra and is verified working ([ESP-Miner #268](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2024-08-05-github-esp-miner-issue-268-firmware-2-1-9-stops-working-afte.md)). bitaxeUltra 205 was released 2025-01-04; ultra-207 (2025-07-02) moved to the TPS546D24A regulator, replacing the TPS40305 / DS4432U / INA260 combination ([Ultra releases](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/github-bitaxeultra-releases.md)), and adds an external MMBT3904 temperature diode next to the ASIC ([ESP-Miner #1228](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2025-09-10-github-esp-miner-issue-1228-add-support-for-bitaxeultra-207.md)). The USB-C CC-pulldown bug (boards not detected on USB-C/Thunderbolt hosts) was introduced on 204 hardware ([bitaxeGamma #37](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/2025-04-28-github-bitaxegamma-issue-37-bitaxe-is-not-detected-when-conn.md)).
- **Ultra Hex.** V300 could not hold voltage under load; V301 held voltage but overheated drastically; V302 is the working version; v303 removed the 33R inline resistors; v304 lacks Vcore output bulk capacitance — fix by soldering two 180 uF polymer caps across the ceramic output caps ([ultraHex README](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/github-bitaxeorg-ultrahex.md)). WantClue said in 2025-04 the Hex had been more or less abandoned ([ESP-Miner #211](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2024-06-10-github-esp-miner-issue-211-hex-2nd-pwm-signal.md)), yet official ESP-Miner later added Hex 303 (v2.11.0) and, per a maintainer, supports Hex 302/303 and Supra Hex 701/702 as of v2.14.0 ([ESP-Miner #1775](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2026-06-18-github-esp-miner-issue-1775-firmware-updates-more-regularly.md)).
- **Supra.** Supra 402+ carries the TPS546 (so AxeOS can show regulator temperature on Ultra 207+, Supra 402+ and Hex 302+) ([ESP-Miner #237](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2024-06-20-github-esp-miner-issue-237-hide-the-axeos-voltage-regulator.md)); the Gamma was derived from Supra 402 hardware ([release records](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/bitaxe-hardware-release-records.md)).
- **Gamma 600 → 603.** gamma-600 (2024-10-10) was the first release: Supra 402 hardware with the BM1370 and a TPS546 with integrated switches. gamma-601 (2025-01-07) added a 4-pin SMD JST fan connector and better fan routing. Gamma-602 (2025-04-07) was a prerelease with DFM and routing improvements. gamma-603 was released 2026-02-17 ([release records](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/bitaxe-hardware-release-records.md)), and ESP-Miner v2.15.0 added board 603 support ([ESP-Miner release records](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/bitaxe-firmware-release-records.md)). The ESP-Miner README notes a USB-C problem on models before 602. skot: no 601 → 602 hardware change should require firmware changes ([bitaxe-raw #9](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/2026-03-03-github-bitaxe-raw-issue-9-having-trouble-flashing-and-runnin.md)).
- **Gamma thermals.** The Gamma draws more power and overheats more easily than earlier models; active cooling with a 5V 4-pin PWM 40mm fan is mandatory — a 12V fan spins too slowly ([bitaxeGamma README](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/github-bitaxeorg-bitaxegamma.md)). A reporter put the Gamma 601 default at around 1.0~1.2TH/s at 525Mhz@1.14V and about 18W ([ESP-Miner #1426](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2025-12-04-github-esp-miner-issue-1426-bitaxe-gamma-601-v2-11-0-self-te.md)).
- **GT 800 vs 801.** Board 800 GammaTurbo units (800x/800xx/800xxx) are development prototypes sold before the official release; they all report as 800, so firmware cannot tell what is on the board, and the official firmware does not support them. Owners' options: return the unit or ask for an 801, revert to the shipped firmware, or get firmware from the manufacturer ([ESP-Miner #1543](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2026-02-06-github-esp-miner-issue-1543-800xxx-support-for-lastest-firmw.md)). The community workaround is flashing the 801 config, which resets settings ([ESP-Miner #1521](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2026-01-22-github-esp-miner-issue-1521-temp-and-volt-issues-on-bitaxe80.md)). The TPS546D24A was disqualified for the multi-phase GT in favor of the TPS546D24S ([ESP-Miner #1255](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2025-09-29-github-esp-miner-issue-1255-remove-support-for-disqualified.md)).
- **Max end-of-life.** A maintainer proposal to phase out update support for the BM1397 Max argued it sold far fewer units than Ultra/Supra and had reached stability ([ESP-Miner #310](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2024-08-28-github-esp-miner-issue-310-phase-out-support-for-bitaxemax-i.md)); bitaxeMax version 103 is untested and some parts are now hard to get ([bitaxeMax #1](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/2025-12-03-github-bitaxemax-issue-1-does-version-103-work.md)).
- **GT "800 triple x".** On POD256 e081 (2025-08-20) Scott described the GT 800xxx revision as two BM1370s with 12-volt XT30 input, a little larger than a normal Bitaxe, with release expected soon. The XT30 is the connector Bitaxes are moving to ([POD256 e081](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-08-20-e081.md)). See the 800 vs 801 note above for how those prototypes are handled in firmware.
- **Gamma Hex.** Per e121 (2026-07-27), the Gamma Hex was championed by Ben from Public Pool. It is nominally around eight terahash but was pushed over 13 at Mining Disrupt on top-bin chips. ## Newer and experimental boards

Status claims in this section are as stated on POD256 episodes and are dated by episode. Several of these boards have no hardware release yet.

### BM1340 / S23-chip experiments (Naja)

- **e081 (2025-08-20).** Scott previewed the Naja prototype, built on two BM1340s out of the S23 and expected to do around five terahash. The S23 die is much larger, roughly fifty watts per chip and normally two and a half terahash per chip. It is rumored, unverifiably, to be a two-nanometer part. Scott said larger dies cost more and suffer more from poor yield, and that S23 chips came from beat-up hashboards and will be very hard to obtain. The names Supra, Gamma and Naja come from a long-time contributor; a naja is a cobra ([POD256 e081](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-08-20-e081.md)).
- **e082 (2025-08-27).** Scott had five chips said to come from the S23, marked BM1340 rather than the BM1372 he expected. Nothing confirmed they were really S23 chips. The chip appeared to share the BM1370 pinout on a different footprint. Started with Gamma (BM1370) register settings on a two-chip Bitaxe, it mined, but reached only about two terahash against an expected five. He blamed register settings he could not discover without a running machine. Because no documentation was available, a friend filmed the log tab of an S23 Hydro's firmware during a demo, and Scott used Grok to transcribe the voltage and frequency initialization values from the screenshots. He asked anyone with an S23 Hydro to capture a logic-analyzer serial dump. The chips were sourced through DTV Electronics ([POD256 e082](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-08-27-e082.md)).
- **e098 (2025-12-13).** Scott said the Bitaxe built with purported S23 chips appeared to outperform the prior generation ([POD256 e098](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-12-13-e098.md)). By e101 the air-cooled S23 had disappeared from Bitmain's site. Scott noted that reverse-engineering S23 chips needs a running machine on a logic analyzer, which is harder with three-phase hydro units ([POD256 e101](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-01-14-e101.md)).

### Bitaxe Raw (USB pass-through)

Bitaxe Raw, by community member K1, replaces ESP-Miner with firmware that passes ASIC, sensor and fan traffic over USB to a PC. The Bitaxe then acts as a dumb hashboard, like an Ember One. Ryan developed Mujina against it. One pitfall: the fan does not turn on by itself, so the board overheated at first ([POD256 e091](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-10-22-e091.md); [POD256 e081](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-08-20-e081.md)). A Bitaxe Gamma over USB is now an officially supported Mujina hashboard ([POD256 e100](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-12-31-e100.md)). It replaced a full-size miner on Ryan's desk for development ([POD256 e095](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-11-19-e095.md)). At BTC++ Nairobi it powered a workshop in which teams fitted heat sinks to bare Bitaxe boards running Bitaxe Raw and drove them from Mujina; Solo Satoshi donated the Bitaxes ([POD256 e118](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-06-24-e118.md)). K1 also wrote the firmware for the ADIT board, a roughly inch-wide USB adapter that presents an Antminer hashboard as a USB serial device ([POD256 e092](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-10-29-e092.md)).

### Bitaxe Bonanza (Intel BZM2)

- **First design (8 chips).** On e081 Scott showed a test board with eight Intel BZM2 chips in a chain, an eleven-to-fourteen-volt XT30 input and a two-phase regulator ([POD256 e081](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-08-20-e081.md)). By e093 (2025-11-05) he said this design did not work, because of bugs plus a basic controller flaw ([POD256 e093](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-11-05-e093.md)).
- **The controller problem.** BZM2 chips use nine-bit serial at a high baud rate. The ESP32 has no native nine-bit serial support, and a workaround failed in testing ([POD256 e093](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-11-05-e093.md)). The BIRDS dev board therefore uses an RP2350, and Scott got the "hello world" chip response on it by e095 ([POD256 e095](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-11-19-e095.md)). The redesigned Bonanza keeps the ESP32 and adds a "sidecar" converter processor between it and the chips ([POD256 e110](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-04-01-e110.md)).
- **Redesign (4 chips).** On e105 (2026-02-18) Scott had finished a four-chip Bonanza design, nominally 1.2 terahash, with a new form factor, heat sink and display ([POD256 e105](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-02-18-e105.md)). The third revision was built and working by e110 (2026-04-01). It takes 12-volt input (not five-volt) and carries an oversized heat sink by Ben for overclocking. - **Status (2026-07 → 2026-08).** On e121 Scott said the board did about one terahash, should reach 1.2, at roughly thirty joules per terahash. Schematics and ESP-Miner changes were published ([POD256 e121](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-07-27-e121.md)). On e123 Johnny had released an ESP-Miner version supporting the Bonanza chips, and Scott expected the Proto and Bonanza Bitaxes to release together ([POD256 e123](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-10-e123.md)).

### Bitaxe Proto (Block's Proto ASIC)

- **e120 (2026-07-15).** Scott's Bitaxe Proto was hashing. It uses Block's Proto ASIC, which comes with datasheets and needs no desoldering. ## ESP32-S3 module requirements and counterfeits

- **Only one module is supported:** ESP32-S3-WROOM-1 type N16R8 (16MB flash, 8MB Octal SPI PSRAM). Modules without PSRAM or with Quad SPI PSRAM will not work with the normal firmware ([ESP-Miner README](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/github-bitaxeorg-esp-miner.md)). The ESP32-S3 cannot auto-detect Quad vs Octal PSRAM, so a board built with the wrong module boot-loops after flashing; buyers often discover a silently substituted module only when an update boot-loops the device with the screen off ([ESP-Miner #826](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2025-04-06-github-esp-miner-issue-826-make-firmware-releases-for-other.md)).
- **How to check:** the good module is marked `MCN16R8`; a known-bad one is marked `M0N16`/`MON16` and often lacks other markings, which suggests a counterfeit. The boot log shows "PSRAM chip is not connected, or wrong PSRAM line mode" ([ESP-Miner #1998](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2026-09-24-github-esp-miner-issue-1998-psram-issue-gamma-601-totally-br.md); [ESP-Miner #1510](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2026-01-15-github-esp-miner-issue-1510-firmware-version-2-12-2-doesn-t.md)).
- **What to do:** no-PSRAM boards are limited to v2.9.0 with data logging disabled; newer firmware cannot run on the ESP32-S3's internal RAM alone, so the fix is replacing the module ([ESP-Miner #826](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2025-04-06-github-esp-miner-issue-826-make-firmware-releases-for-other.md)). Maintainer advice: "If it's hashing, it's hashing." — only swap the module if it is cheap. One early small batch from one vendor used the wrong modules and that vendor fixed affected units ([ESP-Miner #1998](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2026-09-24-github-esp-miner-issue-1998-psram-issue-gamma-601-totally-br.md)).
- The ESP32-C5 (Wi-Fi 6, 5 GHz) was judged not useful for Bitaxe: data volume is tiny and 2.4 GHz penetrates walls better ([ESP-Miner #1420](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2025-12-03-github-esp-miner-issue-1420-esp32-c5-support.md)).
- **Bare-metal limits.** Scott on POD256 e091: ESP-Miner is bare-metal firmware for a Wi-Fi microcontroller that cannot run Linux. It has about eight megabytes of RAM and at most about 16 megabytes of code space. That is why Mujina, a Linux application, cannot run on the Bitaxe's ESP32 and targets larger multi-hashboard systems instead ([POD256 e091](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-10-22-e091.md)). The same constraint drove the BZM2 boards' controller workarounds (see [Bitaxe Bonanza](#bitaxe-bonanza-intel-bzm2)).
- **ESP32-C6.** Hosts on e092 (2025-10-29) flagged Espressif's ESP32-C6 as interesting for future Bitaxe and heater designs: it has a RISC-V core and Zigbee support. The episode notes say it supports five-gigahertz Wi-Fi ([POD256 e092](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-10-29-e092.md)). Scott's argument: open RISC-V cores could reduce reliance on the closed binary blobs that make ESP32-based devices hard to verify.

> **Status: Disputed** — ESP32-C6 and 5 GHz
> The e092 discussion credits the ESP32-C6 with five-gigahertz Wi-Fi ([POD256 e092](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-10-29-e092.md)). The ESP-Miner maintainers' 5 GHz discussion concerns the ESP32-C5 instead ([ESP-Miner #1420](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2025-12-03-github-esp-miner-issue-1420-esp32-c5-support.md)). Best assessment: the on-air claim likely conflates the C6 with the C5. Either way, maintainers judged 5 GHz not useful for Bitaxe.

- **Credentials and access.** Dumping a Bitaxe's NVS over USB revealed the SSIDs and passwords of every Wi-Fi network it had joined, stored in plain text. Scott keeps Bitaxes on a separate network and wants encrypted storage ([POD256 e126](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-09-21-e126.md)). ESP-Miner has no firmware password, so anyone on the same Wi-Fi can repoint a Bitaxe. The NerdQAxe firmware can require a one-time password before changes are saved ([POD256 e121](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-07-27-e121.md)). See [Mining Firmware Security](../industry/mining-firmware-security.md#small-miners-bitaxe-class-devices).

This is the same effort as the grant-funded "MicroBT-based BitAxe variant" in the table above. The grant research names the chip KF1950, and the podcast names only the donor machine (M30).

The earliest derivative-product forks were BitMaker-hub/NerdAxe (forked skot/bitaxe 2022-11-17) and BitMaker-hub/ESP-Miner-NerdAxe (forked 2023-01-24) ([ecosystem discovery](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-github-ecosystem-discovery.md)).

## Clones and unsupported variants

- **LuckyMiner (LV06, LV07, LM06).** Not supported by ESP-Miner. Maintainers call them knockoffs: LuckyMiner "essentially stole the work of the Bitaxe project and turned into their own closed source product" ([ESP-Miner #351](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2024-09-25-github-esp-miner-issue-351-board-version-0-11-vs-board-versi.md)); skot: "LuckyMiner is a scam product and is not supported by esp-miner." ([ESP-Miner #1024](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2025-06-10-github-esp-miner-issue-1024-vin-and-vout-error.md)). A buyer's LM06 boards reporting 0.11 failed while his 204 units worked; skot suggested underclocking because of the small fans ([ESP-Miner #159](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2024-04-04-github-esp-miner-issue-159-no-more-correct-results-after-a-f.md)).
- **Why clones are a support problem:** "Bitaxe being FOSS, there are many vendors that cut corners or make other modifications", which can break new ESP-Miner releases ([ESP-Miner #94](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2024-01-21-github-esp-miner-issue-94-about-board-version-distinction.md)). The 256 Foundation newsletter relays Solo Satoshi's case that clones make undocumented component and firmware changes that volunteers cannot reproduce ([newsletter 2025-05-05](../../raw/history/256f-newsletter/2025-05-05-bitcoin-mining-will-not-be-decentralized-until-it-is-open-so.md)), and reported a miner posing as a Bitaxe exploding in June 2025, blaming BOM swaps to cheaper untested components ([newsletter 2025-07-15](../../raw/history/256f-newsletter/2025-07-15-the-bigger-they-are-the-harder-they-fall.md)).
- **Other undocumented variants:** the JDHX Bit Lottery BM1397 v3.8 has no published documentation and needs an ESP-PROG or USB-serial bridge to recover ([ESP-Miner #88](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2024-01-14-github-esp-miner-issue-88-ota-software-update-gone-wrong-bri.md)); a Max clone without Bitaxe branding was refused support ([ESP-Miner #650](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2025-01-12-github-esp-miner-issue-650-where-can-i-find-the-file-for-the.md)); a GeckoAxe Gamma GT was referred to Gecko support since Gecko may have changed the hardware ([ESP-Miner #1662](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2026-04-13-github-esp-miner-issue-1662-power-asic-temp-and-input-voltag.md)); the Bitaxe Touch's firmware lives in a separate acs-esp-miner repo ([ESP-Miner #1681](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2026-04-30-github-esp-miner-issue-1681-file-too-large-for-partition-www.md)); NMAxe is a separate project not supported by the Bitaxe repos ([bitaxeGamma #51](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/2025-10-30-github-bitaxegamma-issue-51-need-help-recovering-nmaxe-gamma.md)).
- **Closed-source firmware forks** are not taken on: maintainers declined requests from a closed-source LV07 ESP-Miner derivative because its changes are unknown ([ESP-Miner #1457](../../raw/firmware/esp-miner/2025-12-15-github-esp-miner-issue-1457-open-the-password-field.md)).
- **Commercial Bitaxe-based products** also appear: Digital Shovel's BluAx (June 2025) was priced at $99 and draws 18 watts; the newsletter noted "time will tell" whether it honors the license ([newsletter 2025-07-15](../../raw/history/256f-newsletter/2025-07-15-the-bigger-they-are-the-harder-they-fall.md)).
- **Support boundary:** hardware-repo and ESP-Miner GitHub issues are not customer support; buyers contact the seller, DIY builders use the OSMU Discord ([bitaxeGamma #29](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/2025-03-14-github-bitaxegamma-issue-29-bitaxe-gamma-not-hashing.md)). See [Bitaxe Troubleshooting](../getting-started/bitaxe-troubleshooting.md).

## Licensing

- The original skot/bitaxe was MIT licensed (snapshot 2022-11-25) ([MIT license capture](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/2022-11-25-bitaxe-mit-license.md)). OpenSats' 2024 grant listing recorded the repo as GPL-3.0 ([grants research](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-research-proto-braidpool-canaan-grants.md)), and a 2024-12-27 commit changed the hardware license to CERN-OHL-S ([commit records](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/bitaxe-hardware-commit-records.md)).
- As of 2026-10-07, bitaxeGamma is CERN-OHL-S-2.0 (strongly reciprocal) and ESP-Miner is GPL-3.0 ([license records](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/bitaxe-current-repository-license-records.md)); GT, Gamma Hex and Naja Duo READMEs also state CERN-OHL-2-S / strongly reciprocal.
- skot's stance: "Bitaxe hardware and firmware is copyleft open source. If you sell a modified version then publishing the source isn’t optional vibes — it’s the deal." He has publicly claimed that HammerMiner admitted using GPL esp-miner code in closed-source firmware (2026-08-27) and that MagicMiner violates the esp-miner GPL (2026-09-02) — one-sided claims from his posts ([X: skot](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-notable-skot-bitaxe.md)). See also the GekkoScience dispute in [Repair, Supply & Vendors](../industry/repair-supply-and-vendors.md).
- **Trademark.** bitaxe.org states the Bitaxe name and logo are internationally trademarked by bitaxeorg ([bitaxe.org legacy list](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/bitaxe-org-legacy-list.md)); on POD256 #91 (October 22, 2025) skot described two Chinese entities attempting to register the Bitaxe trademark in the US ([newsletter: Assembling Freedom #11](../../raw/history/256f-newsletter/2025-11-24-assembling-freedom-11.md)).
- **USPTO opposition (POD256 e091, 2025-10-22).** Scott's account: two China-based parties, using US-based lawyers, had applied to register Bitaxe with the USPTO. Bitaxe had been an unregistered (TM) mark for over two years, and he had never registered it out of distaste for bureaucracy. Working with a trademark law firm, he filed a formal opposition and applied to register the mark himself. The lawyers' first step was to ask the applicants, through their lawyers, to withdraw. Applicants sign a declaration under penalty of perjury that the mark is theirs. Approval takes roughly eight to ten months, and an unopposed application is generally approved without investigation. Registration requires use in commerce, which the real project has. Scott said there is no "public domain" trademark, so someone has to hold it. The risk from a hostile registrant is takedowns of Amazon listings or GitHub repos, since GitHub tends to comply first. An entity rather than an individual can own the registration ([POD256 e091](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-10-22-e091.md)).
- **International marks.** Per the same episode, Bitaxe trademarks had reportedly already been approved in China to another party, in Germany to a friendly organization, and in the UK to an unknown party. The Madrid system allows streamlined filing abroad from a US registration ([POD256 e091](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-10-22-e091.md)). The outcome of the opposition is not in the corpus as of 2026-10-10.

> **Status: Disputed** — who holds the Bitaxe trademark
> bitaxe.org states the name and logo are internationally trademarked by bitaxeorg ([bitaxe.org legacy list](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/bitaxe-org-legacy-list.md)). On POD256 e091 Scott said the US mark was unregistered, an opposition was pending, and marks in China and the UK had reportedly gone to other parties ([POD256 e091](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-10-22-e091.md)). Best assessment: "trademarked" on bitaxe.org means a claimed (TM) mark, not a completed registration in every jurisdiction.

- **Copyright vs trademark.** Scott pointed out that copyright licenses (MIT or copyleft) are separate from trademark, and MIT grants no rights to the licensor's name or logo. Debian, for example, strictly controls its name while the software stays forkable under another name ([POD256 e091](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-10-22-e091.md)). On e089 a host noted that the copyright holders of a copyleft project, which for Bitaxe means all PR contributors, are the ones with standing to enforce it ([POD256 e089](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-10-05-e089.md)).

## Certification and compliance

As described by Scott on POD256; this is not legal advice.

- **FCC.** The ESP32 module carries its own FCC ID, and the Bitaxe uses the recommended antenna within spec, so the radio is certified at the component level. Part 15 (unintentional radiator) is self-certified. Bitaxe has passed it, and some manufacturers have taken complete products through it. Boards sold as kits put compliance on the end user ([POD256 e093](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-11-05-e093.md)).
- **CE and RoHS.** Bitaxe has passed CE, and EU sellers carry the CE mark. RoHS mainly restricts lead, so many Bitaxe makers use lead-free solder for EU compliance. Scott prototypes with leaded solder because lead-free needs higher temperatures and more process control and produces worse joints ([POD256 e093](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-11-05-e093.md)). On e083 he noted that some Bitaxes have obtained EU regulatory approvals ([POD256 e083](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-09-03-e083.md)).
- **UL / safety.** No safety certification such as UL is legally required. UL and its competitors are private firms, certifications are narrow, the whole system has to be certified, and modifications void it. Open-source hardware ships "as is", but a downstream integrator can certify a whole system, and full source makes compliance changes easier ([POD256 e083](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-09-03-e083.md)). Tyler's counterpoint on e093: certifications matter for home-insurance claims ([POD256 e093](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-11-05-e093.md)).

## Buying: the legitlist

legitlist is the community-verified list of genuine Bitaxe vendors that feeds bitaxe.org/buy: vendors open a PR, the community reviews, maintainers merge, with "No backroom deals. No paid placements." ([legitlist README](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/github-bitaxeorg-legitlist.md)). Approval requires genuine (non-clone) hardware, a live shop, real product photos, product pages linking to bitaxe.org and no license-violating derivatives; selling closed-source miners is fine if they are not Bitaxe derivatives ([vendor guide](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/github-legitlist-vendor-guide.md); [maintainer guide](../../raw/hardware/bitaxe-osmu/github-legitlist-maintainers.md)). The list moved to this GitHub backend, showing the reasoning behind each listing, on 2026-05-06 ([X: skot](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-notable-skot-bitaxe.md)); at the 2026-10-07 snapshot it had 27 vendors, 17 of them in Europe ([legitlist regions](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-github-legitlist-vendor-regions.md)). Process details are in [Repair, Supply & Vendors](../industry/repair-supply-and-vendors.md#open-source-miner-vendors--the-legitlist).
