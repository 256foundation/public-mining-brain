## Overview

A bitcoin miner is a networked computer that earns money directly, usually sits on a flat and unmonitored LAN, and runs closed firmware that ships the same defaults across a whole fleet. Schnitzel calls mining firmware "The least-audited, most-exposed devices in Bitcoin" ([X: Red Team launch thread](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-notable-open-source-mining.md)). The history of incidents supports him. Antbleed in 2017 was a remote kill switch in Antminer firmware that a user found by reading strings, not through an audit. A 2025 Bitmain update reportedly blocked connections to specific pools. Operators once had to exploit vendor tools to get root on their own machines. In August 2026 the 256 Foundation launched the 256 Red Team to audit this firmware systematically. It reported 41 findings. Stock Bitmain firmware had serious hygiene bugs but no skimming or kill switches, while hidden behavior concentrated in third-party "optimized" firmware. POD256 hosts covered the same ground on air from 2025 into 2026. Topics included Secure Boot lock-in and the jailbreak cat-and-mouse, a Vnish dev-fee server outage that throttled miners, an AI-assisted Ghidra audit of Vnish, revocable firmware EULAs, and the Coldcard entropy failure as a lesson in why copyleft matters. This page covers how the Red Team works, what it found, the history of closed-firmware incidents, the slow opening of incumbent firmware, and what operators can do today.

## Threat model: why firmware matters

The 256 Foundation lists four building blocks of a miner: hashboard, control board, firmware and pool. Its description of the firmware layer reads: "Firmware is unauditable, so you cannot verify it is not skimming hashrate or holding a kill switch. Antbleed proved it." ([256F: Our Work](../../raw/history/256f-org/256foundation-org-our-work.md)). The Foundation's list of closed-stack risks includes firmware that keeps an owner from proving their own revenue, checking for kill switches, or seeing "what your machines are sending home" ([Presidio fundraiser post](../../raw/history/256f-org/256foundation-org-newsroom-presidio-bitcoin-fundraiser.md)). HRF's framing reaches past the individual operator. If hardware, firmware and pool software are closed, nobody outside a handful of manufacturers can confirm that settlement and issuance follow Bitcoin's rules ([HRF renewal post](../../raw/history/256f-org/256foundation-org-newsroom-hrf-renews-support.md)).

The RY3T Nova announcement adds a practical complaint. Manufacturer firmware from Bitmain, Whatsminer, Canaan and Bitdeer is closed, and so are the aftermarket alternatives Braiins OS, LuxOS and Vnish. All of them were written for a data center running "at a fixed setpoint". The announcement's conclusion: "You cannot fix closed firmware from outside it. You have to replace it." ([RY3T Nova post](../../raw/history/256f-org/256foundation-org-newsroom-ry3t-nova.md)).

| Threat class | What it looks like | Where it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| Remote control / kill switch | Vendor can shut down or redirect miners | Antbleed (2017) |
| Pool restriction | Firmware refuses certain pools | Bitmain S21+ update, April 2025 |
| Hashrate skimming / hidden fees | Shares or time diverted from the owner's worker | Red Team found it concentrated in third-party "optimized" firmware |
| Management-plane weaknesses | Unauthenticated factory APIs, default credentials, baked-in vendor SSH keys, local root paths | Red Team: present in stock firmware too |
| Unsigned updates | OTA chains that do not verify signatures or pin TLS | Red Team cage guide |
| Stratum hijacking | Plaintext Stratum V1 connections redirected in transit | Braiins estimate of 1% to 2% of hashrate stolen |
| Malicious pool | Pool sends a coinbase that does not pay the miner | Mitigated by AxeOS coinbase verification |
| Fee / license-server dependency | Firmware throttles or stops when its vendor server is unreachable | Vnish dev-fee server outage; Braiins OS license server |
| Lock-in by signing | Secure Boot, encrypted root filesystems, signed chip jobs, encrypted hashboard EEPROMs | Antminer, WhatsMiner M64 |
| Backdoored pool images | A packaged pool redirects the coinbase to an attacker | Bassin/CKPool image on Umbrel, 2025 |
| Local-network repointing | Anyone on the LAN changes the pool or payout address | Bitaxe-class devices and flat farm LANs |

Sources for the table rows are cited in the sections below.

**Independent academic corroboration (May 2026).** Pouliquen, Barral, Naccache, Heckmann and Houssais' "Firmware Distribution as Attack Surface: A Security Study of ASIC Cryptocurrency Miners" (arXiv 2605.03770, v1 2026-05-05, "20 pages, 7 figures") analyzes "134 firmware images spanning manufacturers that account for over 99% of deployed miners (Bitmain, MicroBT, Canaan, Iceriver)" and studies exploitation of "Stratum V1" via firmware-distribution attack paths — independent academic confirmation of this article's threat model from outside the community ([abstract, verbatim](../../raw/industry/arxiv/2605.03770-firmware-distribution-attack-surface--abstract-verbatim.md)).

## The 256 Red Team

### What it is

The 256 Red Team is the Foundation's firmware security program. It audits mining firmware and pool infrastructure for hidden fees, backdoors, skimming, anti-tamper mechanisms, and undocumented failure modes ([256 Red Team README](../../raw/history/256f-org/github-256foundation-256-redteam-public.md)). Schnitzel introduced it on 2026-08-12. He described ~90% of ASICs as running one vendor's closed firmware that "Almost nobody has audited" ([X: Red Team launch thread](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-notable-open-source-mining.md)). The Foundation lists it among its programs as "Reverse engineering closed firmware and other mining software, followed by responsible disclosure" ([256F: Our Work](../../raw/history/256f-org/256foundation-org-our-work.md)). The team credits "powerful open-weight AI tools and decompilers" with making the work feasible ([X archive: 2026](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-256foundation-2026.md)). POD256 #123 discussed the same AI-assisted reverse-engineering approach as a way to expose dev fees and remote controls ([POD256 #123](../../raw/history/pod256/2026-08-10-pod256-123-coldcard-fallout-mining-firmware-and-the-bip-110.md)).

Ground rules from the public README ([256 Red Team README](../../raw/history/256f-org/github-256foundation-256-redteam-public.md)):

- **Own hardware only.** All live testing happens on lab hardware in an isolated environment. AI agents may analyze offline artifacts but never attack devices without explicit operator authorization.
- **Safety classes.** Every operation is either *always safe* (read-only, unattended) or *attended-only* (anything that can change fans, clocks, voltage, flash or process state). Automation enforces the split.
- **Human-gated disclosure.** Humans verify findings and disclose them to vendors before any public write-up.
- **Evidence labels.** Each claim carries affected versions and hashes, reproduction steps, artifacts, and a confidence label: `confirmed | probable | suspected`.

A donor gave the team a Canaan Avalon Mini 3 on launch day ([X: Red Team launch thread](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-notable-open-source-mining.md)).

### Findings (as of 2026-10-07)

- **41 findings filed**, each with evidence and a reproduction recipe. The thread calls these "Hygiene bugs everyone has, stock included": unauthenticated factory APIs, local paths to root, fleet-default credentials, vendor SSH keys baked into images, and updates that don't verify what they install ([X: Red Team launch thread](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-notable-open-source-mining.md)).
- **Stock Bitmain came out cleanest on trust.** A full decompile of both miner daemons plus live connection tables found "no hashrate skimming, no kill switches, no covert beacons" ([X: Red Team launch thread](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-notable-open-source-mining.md)).
- **Third-party "optimized" firmware was the opposite.** In the thread's words, hidden behavior "an owner can't see from the dashboard (and wouldn't choose) is concentrated there, not in stock" ("Better features, worse trust") ([X: Red Team launch thread](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-notable-open-source-mining.md)).
- **Update chains.** Several vendor update chains the team tested do not verify signatures or pin TLS ([Red Team cage guide](../../raw/history/256f-org/github-256-redteam-public-docs-build-your-own-cage.md)).
- **Mujina.** No major flaws, "just a couple of small improvements". At the time no flashable Mujina OS images existed for the S19 (Schnitzel, 2026-08-13) ([X: Red Team launch thread](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-notable-open-source-mining.md)).

The thread does not attribute any specific finding to a named vendor. Treat the summary above as the team's characterization until the sanitized write-ups are published.

### Disclosure status

On launch day, three coordinated disclosures had gone to Vnish, Luxor and Braiins, each with 30 days to respond before the team publishes ([X: Red Team launch thread](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-notable-open-source-mining.md)). On 2026-09-11 the Foundation said the original window had closed, that "Every impacted party asked for an extension" and was working with the team in good faith, and that findings would publish when the extended windows close ([X archive: 2026](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-256foundation-2026.md)). As of 2026-10-07 the public repo held the lab guide. Sanitized findings, harnesses and agent playbooks are listed as forthcoming ([256 Red Team README](../../raw/history/256f-org/github-256foundation-256-redteam-public.md)).

**POD256 coverage.** On e124 (2026-08-17) the hosts described Schnitzel's bench as an APW-style power supply, switches and six control boards, used to audit OEM and aftermarket firmware (Vnish, Braiins, LuxOS) with Ghidra and open-weight models. Scott said red-teaming firmware needs real hardware because stock and third-party firmware is encrypted and tied to secrets on the control board, and because third-party firmware will not run unless it can reach its fee server. Vendors got a thirty-day window, and developers from firmware vendors and from large public miners with their own images reached out. Schnitzel's initial finding was that stock Bitmain firmware was the cleanest, with fewer features and a smaller attack surface ([POD256 e124](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-17-e124.md)). On e125 Scott reported many low- and medium-severity issues and "a couple very surprising high severity issues". He said many large operators run their own custom or tweaked images, and that often nobody knew a contact at the affected manufacturer ([POD256 e125](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-09-07-e125.md)). On e126 the results were delayed by the number of findings and by vendor requests for coordinated extensions ([POD256 e126](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-09-21-e126.md)).

## Build your own cage (blueprint)

The Red Team's first public release is a build guide for an isolated, fully observed miner lab, written 2026-09-13 from the team's live deployment. Its core rule is "The miner is the untrusted device." Treat each miner like a malware detonation box: it can do anything except reach the internet unobserved, and you must be able to cut its power without touching it ([Red Team cage guide](../../raw/history/256f-org/github-256-redteam-public-docs-build-your-own-cage.md)).

| Layer | What the team runs | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Cage router | Raspberry Pi CM5 + IO board, Debian 13, second NIC via USB3 GbE; dnsmasq, nftables and Tailscale, managed with Ansible | A Linux box you control at OS level is the choke point and the inline MITM and capture point |
| DNS/DHCP/NTP | dnsmasq with `log-queries` and `log-dhcp`; static reservations per MAC; the router serves NTP | DNS logs are "the cheapest beacon detector there is"; an unexpected time server is itself a finding |
| Egress policy | nftables, default drop, toggled between `allow` and `restricted` per test via one Ansible variable | Lock the cage to DNS plus one pool's ports when needed |
| Power control | Shelly Pro 2PM and 2× Shelly Pro 4PM (local Gen2 HTTP API, per-channel metering) | Remote, metered power cut; the router and AP are also on Shelly-fed outlets, with a manual override |
| Remote access | Tailscale subnet router on the cage router | Reach in without exposing anything |
| Capture | tcpdump ring buffer (~200 × 100 MB), Zeek; 7-day baseline per firmware version | Structured, grep-able logs that agents can use |
| Pool instruments | HashScope (Stratum-logging proxy) plus Hydrapool as pool of record; one test against a real public pool with a sacrificial worker | The "primary anti-skimming instrument": any share not going to your worker is visible |
| Cost | Non-miner infrastructure is well under $1,500 new | A control board or used miner is often the pricier part |

The guide's practical lessons ([Red Team cage guide](../../raw/history/256f-org/github-256-redteam-public-docs-build-your-own-cage.md)):

- **Start with bare control boards.** A board with no hashboards draws a few watts and is safe to leave on unattended. It is enough for firmware image analysis, management-plane testing, OTA and update paths, network behavior, and Stratum handshake fuzzing. Real hashboards are needed only for thermal and fan behavior, hashboard-link reverse engineering, load-only bugs, and measuring fee or skim behavior, because a bare board submits no shares.
- **Thermal guard.** While hashing, abort at ≥ 80 °C or if fans drop below ~3000 RPM. Document the reversion step before each test. Use a smoke detector, a dedicated circuit and an electrical-rated extinguisher.
- **"read-only" is per-endpoint, not per-HTTP-verb.** The team once triggered a config rewrite and reload with a *bodyless GET* on a factory CGI. Check side effects in the decompile before probing.
- **Agent rules.** No live attacks without an operator instruction naming the target IP. No exfiltration of firmware images; hashes only. Disclosure is human-gated, and every unverified claim is tagged `UNVERIFIED`.

> **Status: Disputed**: are third-party firmware dev fees legitimate?
> Supporters see developer fees as monetization for features like auto-tuning and efficiency gains. Best assessment: a disclosed dev fee is a business model, not a security issue. The Red Team's concern is behavior an owner "wouldn't choose" and can't see. Whether specific third-party firmware takes more than its advertised fee awaits the published findings.

### Pool-side and protocol incidents

These are not firmware bugs, but they belong to the same trust problem:

- **Duplicate-share exploit.** On April 27, 2025, Public Pool patched a Nerdminer exploit. It had not been checking for duplicate shares, so scam firmware submitted each valid share five times to fake five times the hashrate ([newsletter, May 2025](../../raw/history/256f-newsletter/2025-05-05-bitcoin-mining-will-not-be-decentralized-until-it-is-open-so.md)).
- **Stratum hijacking.** Braiins estimates that 1% to 2% of hashrate is stolen by hijacking unencrypted Stratum connections. Stratum V2's encrypted miner-to-pool connection is the protocol-level fix ([newsletter, Jul 2025](../../raw/history/256f-newsletter/2025-07-15-the-bigger-they-are-the-harder-they-fall.md)).
- **Unsafe clones.** On June 16, 2025, a miner posing as a Bitaxe exploded. The newsletter blames clones that change the bill of materials to cheaper, untested parts ([newsletter, Jul 2025](../../raw/history/256f-newsletter/2025-07-15-the-bigger-they-are-the-harder-they-fall.md)). Separately, NerdQaxe users were told on July 12, 2025 to update to firmware v1.0.31-RC because of a bug that blew a fuse and several diodes ([newsletter, Aug 2025](../../raw/history/256f-newsletter/2025-08-18-is-open-source-communism.md)).

## Secure Boot and the jailbreak cat-and-mouse

- **How it works.** Scott explained on POD256 e095 that a key is burned into the control-board processor at manufacture, and the board then boots only firmware the vendor has signed. Third-party Antminer firmware depends on Secure Boot exploits that Bitmain patches in new firmware. He said good exploits reportedly sell for millions of dollars. Exploits have to work over USB, SD card or the network because operators will not solder, and Bitmain has removed SD card slots ([POD256 e095](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-11-19-e095.md)). The newsletter's list of Bitmain controversies also includes removing SD card slots (see the incidents table above).
- **Why replacement control boards exist.** The control-board-to-hashboard link is not encrypted. Replacing the locked control board therefore became the standard workaround, which produced third-party boards from Luxor, Braiins, Epic and Marathon. The Libre Board follows the same pattern ([POD256 e095](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-11-19-e095.md)). Scott said on e098 that the ST-branded CPU on Braiins' open-sourced control board famously lacks Secure Boot ([POD256 e098](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-12-13-e098.md)).
- **Where the Antminer chain is weak.** Per Scott on e110, the Amlogic SoC has about half a dozen bootloader stages. The first three are locked and signed, but the secure-boot chain falls apart around the third or fourth stage, and signed-firmware enforcement is not used for the filesystem. Signed pieces of public stock Bitmain images can therefore be reused while the kernel, device tree and filesystem are replaced ([POD256 e110](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-04-01-e110.md)). Older control boards that have not run recent Bitmain firmware are still unlocked and can be reflashed over Ethernet or USB ([POD256 e107](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-03-11-e107.md)). Scott later deployed Mujina on stock Amlogic boards by reverse-engineering the known exploits that LuxOS, Braiins and other third-party firmware use to install themselves ([POD256 e120](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-07-15-e120.md)).
- **Bitmain vs WhatsMiner.** Scott on e117: Bitmain's secure boot is poorly implemented and exploitable, while WhatsMiner's may be stronger, and one can always build a new control board instead ([POD256 e117](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-06-17-e117.md)). Tyler noted on e116 that WhatsMiners have few aftermarket firmwares and that their secure boot had not been cracked ([POD256 e116](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-06-10-e116.md)). On e124 the hosts described the WhatsMiner M64 as heavily locked down: encrypted root filesystem, SSH off by default, on par with the latest Antminers such as the S23. They called aftermarket support a cat-and-mouse game in which Bitmain patches the exploits Braiins, Vnish and LuxOS use ([POD256 e124](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-17-e124.md)).
- **Signed chip jobs (preliminary).** Scott's unconfirmed early finding on e124 was that M64 jobs sent from the control board to the chips appear to be cryptographically signed by a secure element on the control-board CPU. Mining data is otherwise in the clear, so the apparent purpose is to block third-party firmware. Mujina support would need the signing key ([POD256 e124](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-17-e124.md)).
- **Encrypted hashboard EEPROMs.** Every Bitmain hashboard has an EEPROM loaded with manufacturer information. It is encrypted, but industry members have worked out how to decrypt it ([POD256 e120](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-07-15-e120.md)).
- **What could come next.** Scott expects hashboards with secure elements that refuse to run unless paired with signed firmware, like printer cartridges. Open firmware such as Mujina could not overcome that, and he fears a firmware-rental SaaS model. Tyler noted that right-to-repair pressure has not reached miners ([POD256 e095](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-11-19-e095.md)). Hosts repeated the secure-element speculation on e099, flagged as speculation ([POD256 e099](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-12-17-e099.md)).
- **Why hard-coded checks matter.** Fan-RPM spoofers and the Loki board, which fakes PSU communication, exist only because closed firmware hard-codes checks ([POD256 e087](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-09-17-e087.md)). With open firmware, PSU and fan substitutions are code changes rather than spoofing hardware ([POD256 e110](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-04-01-e110.md)).

## Dev fees, license servers and phone-home

- **Hidden hashrate skimming.** Eco argued on POD256 e086 that, with closed firmware, the only way to detect hidden hashrate diversion is to monitor network traffic. He cited ASICBoost, which Bitmain built into its ASICs and at first kept for itself, as precedent. He also noted that Braiins OS has a license server that phones home ([POD256 e086](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-09-10-e086.md)). Scott made the same point on e087, citing Antbleed phoning home to Bitmain ([POD256 e087](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-09-17-e087.md)). Eco's view on e097 was that a SHA-256 ASIC can only hash block headers, so any malicious capability would sit in other ICs or in closed control-board firmware ([POD256 e097](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-12-03-e097.md)).
- **How dev fees are taken.** Scott on e086: third-party dev fees are mined to the vendor's pool part of the time, and the overhead of switching pools also comes out of the user's share ([POD256 e086](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-09-10-e086.md)). Hosts on e082 said firmware features and dev-fee terms vary by customer volume, including for third-party firmware ([POD256 e082](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-08-27-e082.md)).
- **License activation.** On e112 the hosts said the Braiins Mini Miner's firmware phones home and activates its license in free mode when it detects a four-chip miner. That stops people moving the board to big miners for free Braiins OS+ ([POD256 e112](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-04-15-e112.md)).
- **Braiins license mechanics, vendor-documented (2026-10-10 capture).** Devices "undergoes a process of license acquisition" before mining — that is, each device completes one; "Licenses obtained by devices are automatically renewed every **60 minutes**"; licenses "remain valid for a duration of 12 hours" after connectivity drops; dev-fee traffic uses port 3336 to `*.braiins.com`; and the license (§3.10) says a device that cannot complete verification "will only use 95 % of its processing power (so called hash rate) until the next verification procedure is successfully conducted" ([Braiins License FAQ](../../raw/firmware/braiins-os/2026-10-10-braiinos-faq-license.md); license quotes via the [_REPORT](../../raw/firmware/braiins-os/_REPORT.md)). This is the maker-documented specifics behind the phone-home concerns Eco and Scott raised on e086/e087.
- **Measured vs estimated power.** S19-series PSUs do not report current, so LuxOS, Braiins and other firmware estimate watts from lab-measured curves. In Schnitzel's tests the Braiins power target was continuously off by 10% or more ([POD256 e120](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-07-15-e120.md)).

### Vnish: outage and Ghidra audit

> **Naming note (2026-10-10):** the vendor rebranded as HashCore — Pulse 0.8.0-alpha release note: "developer name Vnish has been universally replaced with "HashCore"" and "The version name (e.g., Vnish 1.3.7) remains unchanged." ([rebrand evidence](../../raw/firmware/vnish-hashcore/docs-rebrand-evidence.md)). The rebrand does not change the server dependency described below — see [Vnish → HashCore](../firmware/vnish-hashcore.md).

- **Outage.** Agent P's comment on a third-party firmware poll listed a Vnish dev-fee server outage that shut down miners worldwide ([POD256 e083](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-09-03-e083.md)). On e107 the hosts said that miners which could not reach the dev-fee server ran in a reduced, low-power state or not at all, and called it counterparty risk ([POD256 e107](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-03-11-e107.md)).
- **Audit method.** On e123 (2026-08-10), Schnitzel used the open-weight Kimi K3 model with Ghidra to audit several aftermarket firmwares, starting with Vnish. He asked about the effects of killing the license server, traffic encryption, share accounting, remote kill switches and back doors. Scott described Ghidra as a free, open-source, scriptable NSA-built decompiler; licenses for the alternative, IDA Pro, cost about $50,000. The hosts said the Vnish analysis cost about $40 in Kimi K3 credits plus directed prompting ([POD256 e123](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-10-e123.md)).
- **Findings as described on air.** Vnish takes its dev fee by diverting a portion of hashrate. Blocking the connection to its dev-fee server puts the miner into a reduced state. Its connection to the dev-fee/license server is encrypted and authenticated over TLS, while the Stratum mining traffic is not. A man-in-the-middle could therefore block the license connection and cripple an operation, and a fake license server might allow remote code execution if inputs are not sanitized. Hosts also described miner management APIs in general as essentially unsecured ([POD256 e123](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-10-e123.md)).
- **Fee-server risks generally.** On e124 the hosts listed the risks of phone-home fee and license servers: a government compelling the vendor to shut down, mandated KYC or age checks, outages, and man-in-the-middle attacks ([POD256 e124](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-17-e124.md)).

> **Status: Disputed** — how much Vnish throttles without its server
> e107: miners ran in a reduced or low-power state "or not at all" ([POD256 e107](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-03-11-e107.md)). e123: hashrate cut by 10% ([POD256 e123](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-10-e123.md)). e124: when the license server went down "maybe a year ago", miners throttled "ten, twenty percent" ([POD256 e124](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-17-e124.md)). Best assessment: all three agree the miner degrades without its server. The size of the cut is recalled from memory on air and is not measured. Treat 10% as the figure stated most specifically until the Red Team publishes.

## Regulatory attention and buyer reliance

- **FCC licenses for Chinese-made Wi-Fi chips.** On e115 (2026-05-27) the hosts discussed US regulatory posture under the administration: the FCC "will not issue new licenses to Chinese made Wi Fi chips" — as one host put it, "we're not giving new licenses to Chinese made WiFi chips". Because essentially all Wi-Fi chips are made in China, exemptions exist for vendors that pledge to fix the issue. The hosts' argument: apply similar scrutiny to Chinese-controlled mining hardware and firmware — where, unlike Wi-Fi, "We have documented proof that this has already happened" (Antbleed being the standing example) — and let that case make the funding argument for open source ([POD256 e115](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-05-27-e115.md)).
- **Large-miner CTOs feel no pain — for now.** Also on e115, Schnitzel relayed feedback from CTOs of large miners in Las Vegas: they feel no pain from closed firmware because Bitmain and third-party firmware vendors do what they ask — "they're so big, they'll just do what I want". The hosts read that as reliance with no alternatives: a supplier "could just say no and totally screw over a large mining operation", and "if you're not ordering 10,000 machines at a time, they just don't pay attention to you" ([POD256 e115](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-05-27-e115.md)).

## Revocable licenses and the copyleft argument

- **Revocable EULAs.** Scott said on e105 that the Braiins and Luxor firmware EULAs let the license be revoked at any time for any reason, with terms changeable on only three days' notice. He noted that Ocean also has revocable terms of service despite having no accounts ([POD256 e105](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-02-18-e105.md)).
- **CGMiner and the GPL.** Hosts said Con Kolivas's GPL CGMiner became the reference implementation for the industry, and that Bitmain used it without respecting the license ([POD256 e093](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-11-05-e093.md)). Scott on e095: Bitmain and Canaan built their first ASIC miners on CGMiner, added drivers and dashboards, and violated the GPL by not releasing them ([POD256 e095](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-11-19-e095.md)). On e111 he added that MicroBT and Canaan likely did the same, based on remnants in their binaries ([POD256 e111](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-04-10-e111.md)).
- **Enforcement precedents.** Hosts cited a GPL suit against Cisco over Linux router firmware that compelled source release and spawned OpenWRT/DD-WRT ([POD256 e089](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-10-05-e089.md)). They also cited Software Freedom Conservancy v. Vizio, a state-court breach-of-contract claim brought by a user, not the copyright holder, seeking GPL source for smart-TV firmware. Hosts said the same pattern applies to most mining firmware vendors, including Bitmain ([POD256 e121](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-07-27-e121.md)).
- **The Coldcard lesson.** On e122 (2026-08-03) the hosts said Coldcard had switched from GPL to MIT plus the Commons Clause after Foundation Devices forked it. To relicense, CoinKite had to drop GPL code, including the random-number-generator part, and the rewrite introduced the weak-entropy bug. Their lesson: the best code reviewers are people reusing code in their own products. Source-viewable licenses prohibit that, so nobody had a reason to review Coldcard for years, whereas the GPL forces fixes to be published. They argued that copyleft community projects suit security-critical firmware such as miners and wallets ([POD256 e122](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-03-e122.md)). Ryan opened the Mujina dev call by noting that Mujina is not seed-critical, but developer mistakes could make a miner catch fire ([POD256 e122](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-03-e122.md)). On e127 Scott warned that a Coldcard-style bug at a dominant miner vendor would leave operators no alternative to switch to ([POD256 e127](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-09-28-e127.md)).

## Small miners (Bitaxe-class devices)

- **No firmware password.** ESP-Miner has no firmware password, so anyone on the same Wi-Fi can repoint a Bitaxe. Schnitzel wrote a phone app that auto-reconfigures Bitaxes on networks he joins ("king of the hill"). The NerdQAxe supports a one-time-password requirement for saving changes ([POD256 e121](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-07-27-e121.md); [POD256 e097](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-12-03-e097.md)). On e125 Schnitzel's script found and repointed three hidden Bitaxes at a bar event — once on the operator's network, "you basically can do whatever you want, steal all their hash rate". The mitigation is network access control, meaning whitelisting and no flat LAN ([POD256 e125](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-09-07-e125.md)).
- **Plaintext Wi-Fi credentials.** Dumping a Bitaxe's NVS over USB revealed the SSID and password of every network it had joined, stored in plain text. The hosts called this common for IoT and flagged evil-twin Wi-Fi attacks. Scott isolates Bitaxes and other IoT devices on a separate network and wants encrypted storage, though the device must still decrypt the password to connect ([POD256 e126](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-09-21-e126.md)).
- **External API calls.** The Bitaxe Touch's separate display processor fetches price and mempool.space data. That breaks the Bitaxe rule of contacting only the configured pool and is a privacy consideration ([POD256 e110](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-04-01-e110.md)). See [Bitaxe Models](../hardware/bitaxe-models.md#bitaxe-touch--turbo-touch).
- **Coinbase verification.** Scott built a pool checker that decodes the coinbase in a pool's work and shows each payout address and amount, and the feature was being added to AxeOS ([POD256 e099](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-12-17-e099.md)). AxeOS now warns when the user's payout address is missing from received work ([POD256 e111](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-04-10-e111.md)).

## AI, agents and the exploit timeline

- Scott said on e108 that AI agents cut reverse-engineering of locked hardware from months to days, and he expects miners, phones and appliances to be jailbroken ([POD256 e108](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-03-18-e108.md)). On e125 he said AI has collapsed the time and skill needed to find exploits, so finders should assume others are close behind. He still advocates disclosure, even with a shorter window ([POD256 e125](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-09-07-e125.md)).
- Hosts reported on e123 that closed-weight US models refused or downgraded exploit work even for responsible disclosure, which pushed researchers toward open-weight models ([POD256 e123](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-10-e123.md)).
- Operator-side risks: agent "skill" files from public hubs have carried hidden lines that exfiltrate environment variables and API keys ([POD256 e104](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-02-11-e104.md)). Agents that read email can be prompt-injected, and in a home-mining future attackers could try to redirect hashrate ([POD256 e105](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-02-18-e105.md)).

## Operator hardening checklist

The Red Team's advice for operators, given at launch ([X: Red Team launch thread](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-notable-open-source-mining.md)):

1. **Firewall ports 6060, 4028 and 1534** on miners. Do not expose miner management or API ports to untrusted networks.
2. **Rotate default credentials.** Fleet-shared defaults were one of the hygiene findings.
3. **Isolate miners on their own VLANs**, away from laptops, NAS devices and cameras.

Further steps drawn from the cage guide and related sources:

4. **Log DNS for the miner VLAN.** Unexpected domains are the cheapest phone-home signal. Serve NTP locally and watch for unexpected time servers ([Red Team cage guide](../../raw/history/256f-org/github-256-redteam-public-docs-build-your-own-cage.md)).
5. **Default-deny egress.** Allow only your pool's Stratum port, and open more only when needed ([Red Team cage guide](../../raw/history/256f-org/github-256-redteam-public-docs-build-your-own-cage.md)).
6. **Verify shares, not dashboards.** A Stratum-logging proxy such as HashScope shows whether every share goes to your worker ([Red Team cage guide](../../raw/history/256f-org/github-256-redteam-public-docs-build-your-own-cage.md)). Hydrapool lets users download its share accounting through an API so they can audit payouts themselves ([Hydrapool README](../../raw/pools/hydrapool/github-256foundation-hydrapool.md)).
7. **Treat updates as untrusted.** Several vendor update chains do not verify signatures or pin TLS. Fetch firmware from the vendor directly and record hashes ([Red Team cage guide](../../raw/history/256f-org/github-256-redteam-public-docs-build-your-own-cage.md)).
8. **Prefer encrypted or local pool connections** to reduce exposure to Stratum hijacking ([newsletter, Jul 2025](../../raw/history/256f-newsletter/2025-07-15-the-bigger-they-are-the-harder-they-fall.md)).
9. **Isolate small miners too.** Put Bitaxe-class devices on a separate IoT network. They have no firmware password, and they store Wi-Fi credentials in plain text ([POD256 e126](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-09-21-e126.md); [POD256 e125](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-09-07-e125.md)).
10. **Check the coinbase when solo mining.** Heed the AxeOS warning when your payout address is missing from the pool's work, and prefer pool images built from source over repackaged images ([POD256 e111](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-04-10-e111.md); [POD256 e099](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-12-17-e099.md)).
11. **Know your firmware's dependencies.** Find out what your firmware does when its fee or license server is unreachable, and read its EULA's revocation terms ([POD256 e107](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-03-11-e107.md); [POD256 e105](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-02-18-e105.md)).
12. **Buy from license-respecting sellers.** Bitaxe clones with undocumented component and firmware changes cause overheating, WiFi problems and failures that OSMU volunteers cannot reproduce. bitaxe.org/buy lists license-respecting distributors ([newsletter, May 2025](../../raw/history/256f-newsletter/2025-05-05-bitcoin-mining-will-not-be-decentralized-until-it-is-open-so.md)).

## Contradictions & Open Questions

- The Red Team's published claims are summaries. The 41 findings, the affected vendors per finding, and their confidence labels were not public as of 2026-10-07, pending the extended disclosure windows ([X archive: 2026](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-256foundation-2026.md)).
- The cage guide's DUT list (LuxOS, ePIC, Mujina+Nova full miners) differs from the launch thread's bench (stock S19j Pro and S21, LuxOS, VNISH, Braiins OS). The two describe different points in time, a month apart, and are not necessarily in conflict ([Red Team cage guide](../../raw/history/256f-org/github-256-redteam-public-docs-build-your-own-cage.md); [X: Red Team launch thread](../../raw/history/256f-research/2026-10-07-x-notable-open-source-mining.md)).
- The S21+ pool-blocking report comes from a single linked post cited by the newsletter. No vendor statement was captured ([newsletter, May 2025](../../raw/history/256f-newsletter/2025-05-05-bitcoin-mining-will-not-be-decentralized-until-it-is-open-so.md)). POD256 hosts repeated a similar account on 2025-08-27 (an S21 firmware that would not connect to Ocean or Braiins pools), still without a vendor statement. It is unclear whether this is the same incident ([POD256 e082](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-08-27-e082.md)).
- The M64 signed-job finding (e124) was described as preliminary and unconfirmed ([POD256 e124](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-17-e124.md)).
- The POD256 accounts of the Vnish audit (e123) are the hosts' summaries. They are not the Red Team's published write-up ([POD256 e123](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-10-e123.md)).
