## Overview

What open-source mining actually costs, why it can't win on $/TH alone, and where its real economic cases are: alternative form factors, heat reuse, and independence from Bitmain's supply chain. The honest baseline from the community's own manufacturing math: even with free ASICs, small-scale open hardware lands at $8-$12/TH against Bitmain's $.50-$1.50/TH — roughly an order of magnitude — so the mission is 'building something Bitmain doesn't control', not price parity [#2183 · 2025-05-16 · Mike Hamilton] [#2180 · 2025-05-16 · econoalchemist].

- **Reference installs.** A car wash whose owners had been "paying 25 bucks a month previously to run propane" now nets "an additional 20 bucks, 15 bucks a day" after the electric bill and stays open all year (00:06:52). A concrete batch plant heats "the 30,000galon water tank with two ASIC miners" — "we poured Bitcoin heated concrete to make a Bitcoin heated sidewalk" (00:07:22). The town of about a thousand people, "two and a half hours from Walmart", now mines in the branch Wells Fargo abandoned (00:07:53)
- **California solar + mining (Troy Cross).** San Diego "Power prices 46 cents a kilowatt hour and rising"; reformed net metering collapsed the residential solar market his company built on. The normie product: "we will sell you power at a 25% discount from the utility rate" off an over-provisioned roof, two Tesla Powerwalls included — "the total sale to the customer has nothing to do with Bitcoin" (00:14:36). The Bitcoiner product: pay utility rate, the company mines on the property, "you get 80% of that" (00:16:43). One Twitter thread drew 500,000 views in 24 hours and about 250 interested homeowners uploading power bills — "you can think of solar as having a marketing problem and Bitcoin as solving the marketing problem for solar" (00:18:47)
- **Intermittency, solved with used machines.** Over-provisioning ("double the amount of of of of power that the house is using") plus battery banks push uptime past solar hours; "there was a brains article a few years ago like you cannot mine intermittently and make money and it was right" — but "if you buy a used machine" you can "make it work" (00:21:53)
- **Constellation Heating's pitch-split (founder introduced as Nicolola Deang; goes by Nicholas).** Residential pool heaters in the "four to 15 kilowatt" class whose owners mostly end up heating their houses; northeastern US power "maybe 15 cents a kilowatt hour" vs Quebec "like the US equivalent of like 6 cents per kilowatt hour" — "sometimes heat is the product, sometimes the Bitcoin is the product. He sells only S21-based systems — "those machines can be like 15, 16, $20,000" — and shares the dev fee from his Luxor-API control software with the installing tradesman so someone local keeps the machines optimal (00:25:28)

Precedent: "Riot had a month you know July a year and a half ago where they made more" money "from demand response participation than from mining Bitcoin" — aggregate home heaters into the same programs and "that advantage disappears" (00:32:44)

## Sell vs hodl for heat miners

- Scripts over historical weekly hashprice/hashvalue data; example — S9 running at 60% power (8 TH) starting 1/1/2017, heat-only 5 months out of the year: ~$3700 if selling sats weekly vs 75.8 M sats (~$76,000) if HODLing [#1613 · 2025-02-07 · Tyler Stevens] [#1614 · 2025-02-07 · Tyler Stevens] [#1629 · 2025-02-07 · context]
- The book-side parameter for exactly this trade-off is the HODL ratio — "represents how much of the newly mined bitcoins a miner plans to hold... a miner that does not sell any bitcoins has a HODL ratio of 100%. A miner that sells most of their bitcoins could have a HODL ratio of 25%." The same chapter's warning for every model on this page: "There's no fixed cost for mining bitcoin. ## Industry baseline: Braiins, Bitcoin Mining Economics (2023)

The published vocabulary and rules of thumb behind everything above: Braiins' *Bitcoin Mining Economics* (Braiins Publishing, book © 2023; underlying data mostly 2021–2023), quoted from the chapter extracts. Community numbers elsewhere on this page are anecdotal; these are the book-era definitions to check them against.

### Hashprice and hashvalue (ch. 14)

- Hashprice definition (verbatim): "hashprice, which represents the $/TH/s/day that a miner earns, i.e. the daily amount of value that a miner will generate for every terahash/second of hashrate that they control." ([Bitcoin Mining Economics ch. 14](../../raw/books/braiins-mining-economics/01-hashvalue-hashprice-rigprice.md))
- Hashvalue is "the BTC-denominated version of hashprice (BTC/TH/s/day) is in fact called hashvalue on Braiins Insights" — hashprice is just hashvalue multiplied by the BTC price, the same split the community's sell-vs-HODL scripts above run on. (pp. 14–15)
- Volatility asymmetry: "hashvalue is far less volatile... impacted only by changes in network difficulty and the block reward value... Meanwhile, hashprice is much more volatile." (p. 15)
- May 2020 was "the steepest drop for both hashprice and hashvalue... a result of the 3rd halving that took place at block height 630,000 and cut bitcoin's block subsidy from 12.5 BTC per block to 6.25 BTC." (p. 15)
- The China-ban episode shows why the two series must be kept apart: "network difficulty dropped from then all-time highs of just over 25 trillion to a low under 14 trillion, sending hashvalue up by nearly 50% in the process", while price fell from "highs just over $60k to the low $30k range" — "even as hashvalue practically doubled in a matter of weeks, hashprice merely recovered to where it had been before the latest 50% price drop." (pp. 16–17)
- Fee context for everything above: transaction fees per block "have been 1-2% of the total block reward for the majority of the time since July 2022." (p. 17)

### Rigprice and the cost to mine 1 BTC (ch. 23)

- Rigprice definition (verbatim): "rigprice: the $/TH capital expenditure for purchasing mining hardware. For example, a 140 TH/s Antminer S19 XP with a rigprice of $35/TH would have a total unit price of $4900 per machine (140 TH/s * $35/TH)." (p. 23) — the same $/TH axis as the manufacturing-cost debate at the top of this page.
- The timing metric: "rig-to-hashprice is the hardware rigprice ($/TH) divided by hashprice ($/TH/s/day)" and "the lower the rig-to-hashprice or rig-to-hashvalue, the better the timing probably is to buy hardware." (pp. 25–26)
- Marginal vs total cost: "The marginal cost to mine 1 BTC stat is computed very simply by dividing your monthly OpEx (all-in electricity costs) by monthly BTC mined (after revenue fees such as pool and dev fees)"; "Total cost to mine 1 BTC includes the marginal cost but adds to it the depreciation of assets" — "$200,000 on hardware that will fully depreciate in 5 years" adds "an additional $3,333 per month ($200,000/60 months)". (p. 27)
- The shut-off rule (verbatim): "Miners don't shut off when their total cost to mine 1 BTC is below the BTC price, only when their marginal cost to mine is below the BTC price". (p. 29)
- The post-halving squeeze, in book terms: "There is a 6-month period after the halving during which the BTC price is below the miner's total cost to mine 1 BTC, meaning that they would record a net loss after depreciation for those months while still bringing in solid profits on their marginal cost to mine 1 BTC." (p. 28)
- The bull-market hardware trap (book vintage, late 2021): buying "22 104 TH/s S19j Pros at $86/TH in December 2021" on "the 12-24 month loans with 8%+ interest rates that were more common at that time" meant that "by Month 8 the BTC price has dropped below their total cost to mine 1 BTC"; "cumulative profit is only $92,000 in Year 1". (pp. 28–29)
- Survivability (book era, early 2023): hashrate "continued climbing to above 300 EH/s at the time of writing even though price remains 70% down from all-time highs"; the miner who "paid $80/TH for some S19j Pros in late 2021" keeps running "as long as their electricity price is below $0.08/kWh, they'll keep their machines brrrring along." (p. 29)
- Bear-market corollary: "Those with strong balance sheets will acquire machines and infrastructure on the cheap from those who are hanging on to the edge of the cliff." (p. 30)

### Debt capital markets (ch. 32–61)

- Hash rate financing: "Hash rate financing is non-dilutive (similar to debt)" and it "can also allow miners to hedge some of their future production" — though "hash rate marketplaces have been 6 - 12 months away for the last 3 or so years." (p. 60)
- The two collateral classes (book era): bitcoin-backed debt at interest rates "ranging from 4 – 12%" that "typically don't amortize"; ASIC-backed debt "typically had 18-month terms for an all-in cost of capital ranging from 13 – 28%". (pp. 42–45)
- LTV logic: "if a lender had to foreclose on an 80% LTV loan and liquidate the collateral, the asset price could fall by 20% before the lender took a hit. In other words, the higher the LTV, the riskier the loan." (p. 40)
- The collateral strings that touch open hardware directly: "loans with ASIC collateral often prevent the use of aftermarket firmware or advanced cooling technologies (e.g., immersion) without lender approval" — lender sign-off as a gate on firmware and cooling freedom. (p. 50)
- Worked case (p. 52): "a new project is built for $2.4m" with "1,000 S19J Pros purchased at $15 / TH ($1.5m in total)", financed by "an ASIC-backed loan with a ~50% LTV ($750k of debt proceeds, prior to a 2% origination fee). We assume this $765k loan has an interest rate of 15% and an 18 month term."
- TLDR (verbatim): "leverage offers miners the ability to amplify returns and expand faster, but it comes at the cost of fragility during bear markets." (p. 56); aftermath (2023): "many lenders have left the asset class either due to poor loan performance or because they've gone out of business altogether (e.g., BlockFi and Celsius)." (p. 61)

### When miners sell (ch. 62)

- Why miners sell (verbatim): "miners sell to cover costs and take profits. Miners' expenses, including electricity and rent, are mostly fiat-denominated, but their revenues are earned in bitcoin." (p. 64)
- Measuring it properly: Coin Metrics tracks "true miner transaction flows by looking at what happens to coins once they're 1-hop away from the original coinbase transaction"; older metrics like "Miners Rolling Inventory" were "actually showing in 99% of cases... the inflows and outflows from mining pool wallets, not miners themselves." (p. 62)
- Accumulation is real but easy to miss: "miners accumulated over 380,000 BTC in the year leading up to the halving. This buildup was mostly confined to 1-hop addresses, so previous methodologies would've missed it." (p. 63)
- The squeeze model: "With difficulty rising (+80%/year) faster than price (+20%/year)... the miner would need to be selling a larger portion of their BTC mined each month to cover costs." (p. 64)
- The bull-market exception: "during intense bull markets... network difficulty rises less quickly than price and miners are able to cover costs with a smaller portion of the coins they mine each month" — and hedging remains rare: "most miners don't employ advanced hedging strategies... even then, selling coins will continue to be how miners realize their profits." (p. 65)

### Transaction-fee luck and the security budget (ch. 135–146)

- Book-era fee share: "the average transaction fee amount per block over the past couple of years is 0.095 BTC—a mere 1.5% of the total block reward" (excluding the ordinal-fueled Q2 2023 fee boom). (p. 137)
- Fee luck defined: "finding a block very quickly after the previous block was found is 'unlucky' because it will result in relatively lower fees... finding a block after a long network-wide block finding drought is 'lucky' because it gives the mempool time to fill up with higher-fee transactions." (p. 139)
- Measured on blocks #764132–#764145: "The average block value in this stretch is 6.44 BTC, or approximately 0.19 BTC average transaction fees per block"; "By far the most valuable block in the batch, #764136, had an extremely long 1-block duration at 59:19 and racked up ~0.456 BTC of fees—over double the average" while "5 blocks were found within 3 minutes of the previous block, and these 5 blocks rank as the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, and 6th least valuable in the batch." (pp. 139–140)
- Why fee luck grows in weight: by 2034 "the block subsidy has halved 3 more times and sits at 0.78125 BTC", turning "the 4.11% difference from the average block value" of epoch 4 into "a 27.24% difference in Halving Epoch 7". (pp. 141–142)
- Who carries the variance: under FPPS "the pools are taking on all the short-term variance risk, which requires deep BTC reserves to remain solvent through bad luck periods" (p. 138) — hence "Pools can do everything by the book and still become insolvent if their reserves are not deep enough to withstand bad luck, especially if they have bad block finding luck and bad transaction fee luck at the same time." (p. 143)
- Security-budget framing: "there is no identifiable 'correct' value in BTC or fiat terms for the block subsidy to be sufficient to ensure bitcoin's decentralization and resistance to attacks" (p. 135), and per the Handbook, "the energy expended will always be determined by the rewards available to the miners of the network, including fees for block space." ([Bitcoin Mining Handbook ch. 18](../../raw/books/braiins-mining-handbook/02-merits-of-proof-of-work.md))
- Attack-cost anchor (Handbook ch. 32, book © 2022; January 2021 data): at 150 EH/s the physical route needed "approximately 1.364M ASICs needed to amass 150 EH/s" and "~4.4 GW of power", with hardware costing "exceed $5.46 billion" — "~$5.5 billion as a conservative estimate at the time of writing" — while attacking "with synthetic hashrate by taking over several pools would be essentially FREE", mitigated because "miners can switch pools in a matter of seconds". ([Bitcoin Mining Handbook ch. 32](../../raw/books/braiins-mining-handbook/04-cost-to-51-percent-attack.md))

## Machine efficiency tiers and unit economics (POD256, 2025-2026)

The podcast's recurring efficiency-and-economics segment, in one place.

- **Where the curve sits (September 2025, Proto launch).** Proto's rig came in "at 14.1, Jules for Terahash" against Bitmain's top of the line at "12 and a half or maybe even lower", with the S23 claimed at "nine and a half" ([POD256 e085](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-09-07-e085.md)). Same episode: wholesale batches of "amp miner" hardware run "10 to 20% of them just, like, won't work or they'll burn out early" ([POD256 e085](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-09-07-e085.md)).
- **Canaan vs the leaders (October 2025).** Canaan's quiet baseboard units already run "18 joules per terra hash", the newly announced A16 is "which is 12 joules per terra hash" (industrial, with chips expected to trickle into home gadgets), and Bitmain's "s 23 is supposed to be nine" — Scott's heat-miner take: "don't care about the difference between nine and twelve jewels per terre hash" ([POD256 e092](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-10-29-e092.md))

That that's 3.6 kilowatts air cooled"; the M76S: "seven kilowatts immersion up to 440 terahash" ([POD256 e098](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2025/2025-12-13-e098.md))

## Dev fees, tuning and the open-firmware business model (2026)

- **Braiins persistent tuning (April 2026).** Braiins OS announced persistent tuning — re-tuning across power set points and temperature instead of one run. On the pod, Eco described conventional tuning runs that "can take quite a long time, sometimes upwards of an hour plus", and Scott noted sweet spots drift with chip aging, dust and temperature, so guess-and-check probing costs efficiency. The implementation is closed: "It's definitely closed source" — precisely the surface Mujina's open tuning hooks open up ([POD256 e112](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-04-15-e112.md)); several auto-tuning packages "have shown up" for Bitaxe already ([POD256 e112](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-04-15-e112.md))
- **The Braiins Mini Miner's dev-fee-free license.** Dev-fee free on any pool because its firmware "phones home to brains, and then the license activates in free mode" when attached to "a four chip miner" — encoded to stop people moving the board to bigger miners; Braiins OS is only dev-fee free when mining to Braiins Pool, while Mujina "does not have a dev fee" and can mine anywhere ([POD256 e112](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-04-15-e112.md))
- **The Vnish teardown (August 2026).** The 256 red team's first public target: Vnish "take a portion of your hash rate" as the dev fee, and blocking the fee server puts the miner in a reduced state — "reduce your hash rate by 10%", "which in many mining cases means it's just not profitable" ([POD256 e123](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-10-e123.md)). The audit itself cost "$40 and Kimi K3 credits" plus directed prompting — closed-weight model APIs make you "rack up $30 in tokens from, a single prompt" — and the hosts' conclusion is that tuning "special sauce" is now extractable, eroding the case for dev-fee firmware; solutions that "don't require a dev fee" already exist ([POD256 e123](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-10-e123.md))
- **The fee-server risk in one line (August 2026).** Vnish's license server has already gone down once — without it the firmware "throttles down like ten, twenty percent"; Scott's summary: "If we can't get our 2% cut, then you can't have 10% of your hash rate" ([POD256 e124](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-17-e124.md))
- **"Red Hat" service companies (July 2026).** Schnitzel (funded by RY3T for Nova) predicts custom open-firmware service shops as the business model — "making real money on top of free software" — and not just one of them: "The Red Hat is one of them"; "There is thousands of Red Hats in the world", likely specialized per use case ("Red Hat for solar power or for district heating") ([POD256 e120](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-07-15-e120.md))

- Why miners skip nodes: "it's fairly expensive to set up a full archival node due to the storage requirements, and it doesn't really get you anything in your mining adventure" — "it doesn't get you any more like lottery tickets" — so at "$600" most people pass ([POD256 e124](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-17-e124.md)); Eco's phrasing: "it doesn't gain you any more lottery tickets" ([POD256 e123](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-10-e123.md))

We have confirmation from doctor Konkolovas" (Konkolová; Eric Voskuil of Libbitcoin concurred) ([POD256 e123](../../raw/history/pod256/transcripts/2026-10-07/transcripts/2026/2026-08-10-e123.md)).
